WinterGateIC (WIC) has faced sustained, multi-vector attacks since its emergence in July 2026. The primary aggressor is the Outlaw Hacking Group (aka Dota, Shellbot, Dota3), operating the "mdrfckr" SSH brute-force and cryptomining botnet — a financially motivated criminal operation active since 2018 with over 13,000 compromised servers globally and 46+ million recorded sessions from 270,000+ unique IPs.

Secondary activity includes state-aligned retaliation from Iran and Russia, and significant "noise" from legitimate research scanners (Shodan, Censys, Onyphe, Cortex Xpanse) being swept into the same reporting net. WIC's AbuseIPDB reporting is confirmed as accurate by independent OSINT and is contributing to rapid blacklisting of hostile infrastructure.

The Outlaw group is attributed to Romanian origin, operates with surgical speed (persistence achieved within 22 seconds of login), and uses modular, evolving tooling that has undergone multiple documented generations since 2018. Its signature is the mdrfckr SSH key — a stable, globally unique identifier that has remained unchanged since 2018 and is the most prevalent malicious SSH key in academic datasets.

This report serves three audiences:
Defenders who need to detect and block this botnet.
Researchers who need to understand its evolution and infrastructure.
Operators who need to understand what is targeting their environment and why.

This is not a marketing document. This is a working intelligence product. Every claim is sourced. Every indicator is actionable. Every uncertainty is marked.


SECTION 1: THREAT LANDSCAPE OVERVIEW

1.1 The Current Environment

WinterGateIC operates in a threat environment that has three distinct layers, each with different motivations, capabilities, and persistence models.

Layer 1 — Mass Criminal Botnets

These are automated, financially motivated operations that attack anything reachable. They do not target specific victims; they target vulnerable credential sets and exposed services. The mdrfckr botnet is the primary representative of this layer in the WIC threat environment.

Key characteristics:
Automated and scripted.
Opportunistic, not targeted.
Persistent through stable infrastructure.
Financially motivated.
Active for years, not days.
Volumetric and continuous.

Layer 2 — State-Aligned Retaliation

These are coordinated campaigns launched by nation-state actors or their proxies in direct response to specific actions. WIC's public documentation of Iranian and Russian hosting infrastructure provoked a coordinated retaliation campaign on July 14, 2026.

Key characteristics:
Directly targeted.
Coordinated across multiple vectors.
Resourced and sustained.
Motivated by political or strategic retaliation.
Often uses shared infrastructure across allied states.

Layer 3 — Ideological Harassment

These are campaigns launched by ideologically motivated groups — not for financial gain, but for disruption, harassment, or retaliation. UTTP and its affiliates, along with 764-linked actors, represent this layer.

Key characteristics:
Directly targeted.
Emotionally and ideologically motivated.
Often lower sophistication but high persistence.
Frequently uses social engineering and harassment.
Publicly visible, unlike criminal or state activity.

Layer 4 — Background Noise (Not a Threat)

Legitimate research scanners (Shodan, Censys, Onyphe, Cortex Xpanse) generate massive volumes of scanning traffic that is indistinguishable at first glance from malicious activity.

Key characteristics:
Not malicious.
Operating within normal research parameters.
Must be excluded from blocking decisions.
A significant source of false positives in automated reporting.

1.2 Why This Distinction Matters

Automated threat intelligence systems that do not separate these layers will produce noisy, unreliable outputs. A report that treats Shodan the same as Outlaw is not accurate intelligence — it is untriaged data.

The purpose of this report is to enable defenders to separate the signal from the noise and act on the actual threats.

SECTION 3: CULPRIT DESCRIPTION — OUTLAW HACKING GROUP

3.1 Identity and Origin

The Outlaw Hacking Group (also tracked as Dota, Shellbot, and Dota3) is a Romanian-origin, financially motivated cybercriminal organization first identified by Trend Micro in November 2018. The group is believed to originate from Romania based on:

Language artifacts in malware.
Tools used across campaigns.
Hosting and infrastructure patterns.
Historical C2 geolocation.
Romanian-language strings in early versions.

The group should not be confused with state-sponsored actors. It is a criminal enterprise. Its goal is money, not intelligence or disruption for political reasons.

3.2 Operational Model

Outlaw operates a self-propagating, auto-replicating cryptomining botnet that targets Linux and IoT systems through opportunistic SSH brute-force attacks using weak or default credentials.

Primary motivation: Financial gain through Monero cryptocurrency mining.

Secondary revenue streams:
DDoS-for-hire services.
Credential theft and resale.
Possible botnet rental to other criminal actors.

Attack model:
Scan for exposed SSH services.
Attempt credential brute-force with known weak/default credential lists.
On success, immediately establish persistence via SSH key injection.
Disable defensive measures.
Deploy mining payload.
Use the host for further propagation.
Maintain persistence through cron jobs and stable SSH key.

The botnet does not need to be told to attack specific targets. It propagates because propagation is its function. Every host it compromises becomes a new launch point.

3.3 Signature — The mdrfckr SSH Key

The group's most distinctive indicator is the mdrfckr SSH public key, written to ~/.ssh/authorized_keys on every compromised host. This key is the single most reliable indicator of Outlaw compromise.

Key facts:
SHA-256 hash: a8460f446be540410004b1a8db4083773fa46f7fe76fa84219c93daa1669f8f2
Unchanged since: 2018
First associated with Outlaw by: Trend Micro (November 2018)
Global compromised servers: 13,000+
Prevalence: Most prevalent malicious SSH key identified in the Max Planck Institute's dataset
Detection: Any host with this key in authorized_keys is compromised

The stability of this key is unusual. Most criminal groups rotate indicators regularly to avoid detection. The fact that Outlaw has used the same key for eight years suggests either operational laziness or a calculated decision that the key is not being actively hunted.

This report exists, in part, to change that calculation.

3.7 The 22-Second Compromise Timeline

On May 23, 2026, an automated actor successfully authenticated to a Cowrie SSH honeypot using weak, commonly leaked credentials. Within 22 seconds, the actor achieved complete compromise.

Timeline:
0 seconds — Authentication via weak/default credentials
2 seconds — Injects malicious SSH key (mdrfckr) into authorized_keys
4 seconds — Changes root password to generated value
6 seconds — Clears /etc/hosts.deny to remove defensive blocks
8 seconds — Executes 18 system reconnaissance commands
12 seconds — Downloads dota3.tar.gz payload via SFTP
16 seconds — Extracts and executes mining payload
22 seconds — Full persistence established; C2 communication begins

This 22-second window is faster than most incident response teams can react. By the time a human analyst notices the alert, the system is already compromised, persistent, and mining cryptocurrency for the attacker.

The implication for defenders: detection must be automated. Manual response is too slow.

SECTION 9: DETECTION AND HUNTING GUIDANCE

This section is written for defenders who need to act on the intelligence in this report. It is not theoretical. It is operational.

9.1 Immediate Actions

1. Check all Linux hosts for the mdrfckr SSH key in authorized_keys.
Command: grep -r "mdrfckr" /home/*/.ssh/authorized_keys /root/.ssh/authorized_keys 2>/dev/null

2. Check for the known SHA-256 hash.
Command: sha256sum /home/*/.ssh/authorized_keys /root/.ssh/authorized_keys

3. Check for the kswapd0 binary in non-standard locations.
Command: find / -name "kswapd0" -type f 2>/dev/null

4. Check for the dota3.tar.gz payload.
Command: find / -name "dota3.tar.gz" -o -name "tddwrt7s.sh" -o -name "min.sh" 2>/dev/null

5. Check for unusual cron jobs.
Command: crontab -l; ls -la /etc/cron.*/*; grep -r "base64" /etc/cron.*/ 2>/dev/null

6. Check for IRC connections on port 443.
Command: netstat -tunap | grep ":443" | grep ESTABLISHED
Look for connections to eleethub.com domains or known C2 IPs.

9.2 Network-Level Detection

Block the following at the network edge:
eleethub.com domains
Known C2 IPs (45.9.148.99, 45.9.148.117, 54.37.70.249, 185.216.134.126, 163.7.8.79)
IRC traffic on port 443 where the destination is not a known legitimate service

Monitor for:
SSH brute-force patterns across the subnet.
Unusual outbound connections to low-reputation hosts.
XMRig mining pool traffic.

9.3 Host-Level Detection

Deploy Sigma rules for the indicators in Section 8.7.

Deploy YARA rules for the shellbot Perl backdoor and XMRig miner.

Enable SSH key logging on all Linux hosts to detect unauthorized key injections.

Enable file integrity monitoring on /etc/hosts.deny, /etc/cron.*/, and ~/.ssh/authorized_keys.

9.4 Response Playbook

If a host is confirmed compromised:
1. Isolate the host from the network immediately.
2. Preserve forensic evidence before remediation.
3. Remove the mdrfckr key from all authorized_keys files.
4. Identify and terminate all suspicious processes.
5. Remove all unauthorized cron jobs.
6. Rotate all credentials on the host and any connected systems.
7. Rebuild the host from a known-good image if possible.
8. Scan adjacent systems for the same indicators.

This report is one small step in that direction.


SECTION 11: GLOSSARY

Botnet: A network of compromised computers controlled by a single actor, used to conduct coordinated attacks or perform distributed tasks.

C2 (Command and Control): The infrastructure used by an attacker to communicate with compromised systems.

Cryptomining: The use of computing resources to mine cryptocurrency. In the context of criminal botnets, it refers to the unauthorized use of compromised systems to mine cryptocurrency for the attacker.

HASSH: A fingerprinting method for SSH clients, analogous to JA3 for TLS.

IOC (Indicator of Compromise): A technical artifact that indicates a system has been compromised.

mdrfckr: The SSH public key comment string used by the Outlaw Hacking Group as a persistence mechanism.

Persistence: The ability of an attacker to maintain access to a compromised system across reboots or credential changes.

Shellbot: A Perl-based IRC bot used by the Outlaw group as a backdoor and command-and-control mechanism.

TTP (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures): The behavioral patterns of a threat actor, as distinguished from technical IOCs.

XMRig: An open-source Monero mining application. In the context of the Outlaw botnet, it has been modified and packaged as part of the payload.
